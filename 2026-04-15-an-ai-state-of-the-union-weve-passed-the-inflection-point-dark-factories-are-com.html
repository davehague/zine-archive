III

The Great Divide

Vibe Coding vs. Agentic Engineering

Andrej Karpathy's original definition of vibe coding: you don't even look at the code. You go on vibes. You say "build me something that does X" and it builds it. If it looks good, great. If not, you iterate. It's hands-off, and non-programmers can now tell Claude what to build.

Simon loves the democratization. But he draws a sharp line: if the only person who gets hurt by bugs is you, go wild. The moment your bugs might harm somebody else, you need to step back.

For professionals building production software, he's coined a different term: agentic engineering. The art of getting really good results from coding agents—software you could deploy to a million people—is never going to be easy or trivial.

"The moment vibe coding means everything that touches AI, it effectively means programming—because we're all moving in a direction where our code is mediated through AI."

— Simon Willison

Simon is publishing an agentic engineering book one chapter at a time on his blog—the best form of writing, he says, because he has no editor or publisher pressure.

IV

The Next Frontier

Dark Factories Are Coming

The dark factory pattern borrows from manufacturing: if your factory is so automated you don't need people, you can turn the lights off. The machines operate in complete darkness.

For software, this means: nobody writes code and nobody reads the code. But you're not vibe coding. You're applying professional practices and quality expectations to code you're not directly reviewing.

StrongDM pioneered this approach, using swarms of AI testers simulating end users 24 hours a day—complete with simulated Slack channels, simulated Jira, and simulated Okta, all built by their own coding agents.

Their simulated QA team: AI agents in a fake Slack channel, making requests like "Hey, could somebody give me access to Jira?" Running around the clock. StrongDM was spending roughly $10,000 a day on tokens to simulate all of these end users.

"Similar to having a manual QA team, except one that never sleeps."

VI

Career Implications

AI Is Coming for the Middle

ThoughtWorks assembled engineering VPs from multiple companies and arrived at a striking conclusion: AI is great for experienced engineers—it amplifies their skills. It's great for new engineers—Cloudflare and Shopify both hired a thousand interns over the course of 2025 because AI assistants cut onboarding from a month to a week. The problem is the people in the middle. Mid-career engineers who haven't made it to super senior yet, but aren't new either. They don't have deep expertise to amplify, and they've already gotten the beginner boosts.

It's not just engineering. AI is coming at the middle of the product development process, the middle of seniority, and probably every function. New PMs and designers are ramping up more quickly because they're AI-native. Seniors can leverage decades of pattern recognition. The middle cohort faces the hardest transition.

Simon's advice: lean into it. Use AI to amplify your own skills, learn new things, take on more ambitious projects. The only universal skill right now is being able to roll with the changes. And weirdly, the term that comes up most in these conversations is agency—human agency. Decide what problems to take on. Invest in your own ability to choose.

Cloudflare & Shopify each hired 1,000 interns in 2025. Intern onboarding cost went from one month to one week with AI assistants.

VII

The Paradox

11 AM
Wiped Out for the Day

AI is supposed to make us more productive. It's supposed to give us more time off. But the people most AI-pilled are working harder than they've ever worked.

Simon runs four agents in parallel working on four different problems. By 11 AM, he's cognitively done for the day. There's a limit on human cognition—how much you can hold in your head at one time. It's very easy to pop that stack.

People are losing sleep. They're setting off coding agents at midnight, waking up at 4 AM. There's an element of gambling and addiction to how we're using these tools.

"This year, my New Year's resolution was the opposite of every other year. Instead of 'focus more, take on less'—it was take on more stuff and be more ambitious. We've got these tools. Bring it all in."

More Ambitious More Exhausted More Fun

But there's a flip side people haven't mentioned enough: it's actually really fun. Simon's friends have been clearing entire backlogs of side projects they've accumulated over 10-15 years. Some of them feel a sense of loss—their backlog is gone. Now what?

VIII

Benchmarks & Whimsy

A Pelican Riding a Bicycle

Frustrated by numeric benchmarks that don't really tell you anything interesting, Simon started his own: generate an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle. Not a test of image models—a test of LLMs generating SVG code. And something weird happened: there appears to be a very strong correlation between how good a model's pelican-on-bicycle drawing is and how good the model is at everything else.

Nobody can explain why. But the better models really do draw better pelicans. It's reached meme status. The AI labs all relish how good their pelicans are. When OpenAI released GPT 5.4 mini and nano at five different thinking levels, Simon produced a grid of 15 pelicans. Sure enough, GPT 5.4 at extra-high thinking drew the best one.

Simon even has secret alternative prompts in his pocket—an ocelot on a moped, for instance—to detect if labs are overfitting to pelicans. When Gemini 3.1 launched with an animated pelican riding a bicycle in their promotional video, he thought he'd caught them. But they did all the other animal-vehicle combinations too.

Why pelicans? Simon lives in Half Moon Bay near the world's second-largest roosting colony of California brown pelicans. He just likes pelicans.

"All I want from life is a really good picture of a pelican riding a bicycle. If I can trick every AI lab in the world into cheating on benchmarks to get it, that just achieves my goal."

IX

Security Warning

The Lethal Trifecta & the Challenger Disaster of AI

Prompt injection is the unsolved crisis of AI applications. And Simon believes a catastrophic incident is inevitable.

The Three Legs

1. Private Data — The agent has access to sensitive information like your inbox.

2. Malicious Input — An attacker can get their text into your system (e.g., sending you an email).

3. Exfiltration — The agent can send data back to the attacker (e.g., forwarding an email).

You can get to 97% effectiveness on injection filters. Simon considers that a failing grade. Three out of 100 attacks steal everything. Until it's 100%, it just gives people a false sense of security.

"Every single time you get away with launching a space shuttle without the O-rings failing, you institutionally feel more confident. We've been using these systems in increasingly unsafe ways. This is going to catch up with us."

The concept comes from the Challenger disaster's "normalization of deviance." People knew the O-rings were unreliable, but kept launching. Simon has made this prediction every six months for three years. It hasn't happened. That's exactly the pattern that should scare us most.
