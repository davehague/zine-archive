II
II · The problem

You can't read it anymore

“With traditional software, you can read the code and you can tell with some degree of reliability what exactly that code is going to do. With agents, you have no idea.” Laurie Voss of Arize AI, opening his workshop.

Same inputnot the same output
Same outputmaybe not the same path
Every callLLM, tool, agent turn: a trace

“Your code isn't the source of truth for what does my agent do. Instead, the traces are your source of truth.”

Laurie Voss · Arize AI

And not one trace: “your traces at scale are the source of truth,” because without them “you are essentially flying blind when you are building agents.”

▶ 1:38 · ✂ cut · fit 60

The bill arrives at review

Gabriel Martinez, an engineering manager at G2i, on what the speed actually buys: “The output arrives with the shape of completion, but the real work has just moved to the reviewer.”

“Generation is cheap, but evaluation is expensive.”

“If AI produces more than humans can possibly evaluate, it has not accelerated the team. It has created a bottleneck.”

▶ 3:14 · ● in queue · fit 70

So the job becomes judging. The rest of this issue is how to judge well, and then how to judge the judge.

III
III · Hamel Husain ranks the field

S tier: look at the data

Hamel Husain has worked on evals with more than fifty companies. Asked to rank every eval idea from S down to F, he keeps putting the same habit at the top.

S

Error analysis.“A structured approach to looking at your data quickly so that you can find what's broken.”

A custom annotation tool.Build one with Claude or Codex that “removes all the friction from looking at your traces.”

Reading traces yourself.“There's nothing more valuable than looking at the data.”

A

Binary pass/fail.“Binary is just going to cut through a lot of noise.”

C

Writing evals before building.“There's infinite surface area of things that can go wrong.”

D

Evals that always pass.“They're not telling you anything at all.”

F

Off-the-shelf LLM judges.“I challenge you to go read those prompts.”

Prompt optimisation without evals.“What are you even optimizing against if you don't have evals?”

The unit-test trap

“You should think of evals a little bit different than your unit tests in software. Like you don't want them to always be passing.”

A suite that is 100% green has stopped teaching you anything. Keep a few hard cases you are still losing.

V · Meet the judge

Every judge has a type

Laurie Voss lists the known ways an LLM grading another LLM goes wrong. Nufar Gaspar, on The AI Daily Brief, gives the last one a better name.

01

Position

Shown two options, the judge “tends to favor whichever comes first.” Some models prefer the last, “but it always has some kind of preference to position.”

02

Length

“LLMs like longer responses.” They score them higher “even if the… extra length is just filler.”

03

Confidence

The one he loves: “The judge gets fooled by a response that sounds confident just like humans do.”

04

Self-preference

“If you use the same model to do the generation as you use to do the judging, they tend to like their own output.”

The fix, Voss

Use one model for the agent and another for the judge. He graded Haiku's output with Sonnet, “but it works even better if you use a different labs model entirely.”

So she judged with a family she wasn't testing: “because I haven't evaluated anything with Gemini, the judge was a Gemini model.”

VI · How to ask the question

Yes or no beats one to five

39%

“Pointwise is not which dish do you want. Pointwise is do you want food, yes or no.”

Tejas Kumar · IBM

In one study he cites, pairwise comparison “was 39% more vulnerable to attack vectors than pointwise,” and pointwise “fell apart just like five times.”

PASS / FAIL

Scales hide the hard part

“What is the difference between a three and a four? You would have to write a whole bunch of rules for the LLM to know that.” Laurie Voss. Default to zero or one; a 0.5 for “a half-ass job” if you must.

Make it think first

“Ask the judge to reason before it scores.” It “measurably improves accuracy,” and the explanation helps you too. “Just one of those weird things about LLMs.”

▶ 1:12 · ● in queue · fit 62

And scales cost teams

Hamel Husain: a score “hides a lot of complexity in it, and it becomes exponentially more expensive to align your team on what a one, two, three, four, five means.”

The exception: your own taste

Nufar Gaspar, judging models for her own work: “it's often very difficult to judge something as a standalone… What is often easier is to see side by side several alternatives.” So machines grade one answer at a time; your own taste is easier side by side.

VII
VII · Judge the judge

Who checks the checker?

Humans are the benchmark, and humans are bad at this too. Laurie Voss: two experts with the same output and the same rules agree surprisingly little; human inter-rater reliability “is often as low as” 0.2 or 0.3. A judge that beats that is doing better than people.

“What you're looking for is a judge that disagrees with you all of the time. That is the danger signal.”

One that disagrees some of the time “is a judge correctly handling a genuinely ambiguous task.”

He leaves the room with a principle he credits to Anthropic's eval teams: failures should seem fair. “If you look at a failing trace and you think that answer looks fine to me, the problem is probably the eval and not the agent.”

▶ 1:20 · ✂ cut · fit 54

Hamel Husain says the same thing harder. Writing a judge prompt and assuming it works “would be the same thing as just praying that the eval is good.” Label data by hand, then split it:

20 train
40 dev
40 test

Examples for the prompt come from train. Hill-climb the judge against dev. Hide test from yourself until the very end. Measure TPR and TNR, not just accuracy.

▶ 2:38 · ● in queue · fit 76

The AI Automators, fine-tuning a small model on about nine thousand support chats, call this “the most important part,” and it happens “before you train anything.” Four piles: 5,900 to study, just under a thousand to quiz after every pass, a thousand to tune confidence, and a thousand that “play no part in anything until the very end.”

“If you test a model on chats it's already trained on. It'll just remember the answers.”

▶ 1:15 · ✂ cut · fit 46

And the blunt version, from Nufar Gaspar: “if the judge is completely not correlated to your taste, it's a bad judge and just throw it away.”

Your benchmark, not theirs

11 daysNathaniel Whittemore's estimate of the average gap between frontier model releases. Each one ships with a benchmark table.

Why the tables tell you little

Not because anyone is lying: “most of these benchmarks are now in the training datasets,” and “the longer they've been around, the less value there is.” Even perfect benchmarks miss how a model fits your stack: “there are going to be times, I guarantee where the thing that is technically state of the art on some benchmark is worse at the version of the thing that you're doing.”

“There's kind of no shortcut solution to figuring out how a new model is or isn't going to fit into your process other than using it.”

▶ 1:28 · ✂ cut · fit 45

Nufar Gaspar's five steps

  1. Pick the tasks. “Arguably the most important step”: a diverse set you would act on.
  2. Pick the candidates. A model, or a new harness or tool with a model.
  3. Run it. By hand, with a small script, or on a platform.
  4. Hide the names. “We are very biased towards our beloved tools.”
  5. Decide. A win “does not necessarily mean that automatically you need to make changes”: cost, latency, or too small a delta.
▶ 2:28 · ● in queue · fit 74

Keep it fair

Freeze everything: “the exact same request to each and every one of the models.” And run it more than once, because the same model with the same prompt gives different results. “Running it several times will seal the deal.”

When to train your own

The AI Automators' small Laya model, about 420 million parameters, “fits very well anywhere you make the same call over and over again where the available choices are reasonably stable and you have past examples of the right answer.” Trained on your data, it can outperform a model like Jev.

X · Closer

Measure what matters

When the evals say the agent is failing, Laurie Voss has an order for where the effort goes. It starts where this issue started: the data.

1

Data quality

Stale knowledge base? “No amount of prompt iteration is going to help.”

2

Prompting

Few-shot examples, explicit constraints. “Often the highest ROI.”

3

Model choice

Sometimes it solves what prompting can't, but it “will also cost more and be slower.”

4

Temperature, top-p

Easy to tweak, seldom a big lever. “The last thing you try.”

▶ 1:02 · ✂ cut · fit 58
Outcomes, not tokens

Davis Palmie of Factory: token leaderboards are “Goodhart's law in real time.” Count signal-to-production time, human interventions, median time to repair, shelf life of code, cost per PR. “Don't token max.”

▶ 1:38 · ✂ cut · fit 58
Outcomes, not certainty

Jake Van Clief to a student who tripled his company's revenue and still felt he was building the wrong thing: “Put pressure on am I getting an outcome and am I building it in a way where I can fix it later.” “Outcomes matter more than is it right.”

▶ 2:30 · ✂ cut · fit 46
Write down what right means

Runtime Verification, on The Cognitive Revolution: writing the spec is where bugs surface, “almost every time.” Their cheap version: once the code exists, ask the agent to write the spec and prove it. “It's like a review plus plus.”
