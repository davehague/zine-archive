Tip One

Start With One Agent. Give It a Name.

People see flashy screenshots on X—six-agent teams, twelve agents running in the background—and they want to start there. Don't. Start with one agent and make your way up by talking to it. That's the best thing OpenClaw has done: you don't need to be a coding ninja to run these agents.

Shubham names his agents after characters from Friends and The Office. It started as a joke, but it turned into something powerful: personas create mental models. Monica runs operations. Dwight handles research. Kelly drafts social posts. Ross reviews pull requests.

“Having personas creates a mental model. This person's a producer, this person's a fact-checker, this person's an engineer. That does help humans figure this out.”

Tip Two

Onboard It
Like a New Hire

People operate in two extremes: they either give their agent zero context or dump ten folders of files on day one. Both end in disappointment. The sweet spot is the same way you'd onboard an intern.

The Wrong Way

Dumping every company document, wiki, and internal memo into the agent's context window on day one. The model does a context overload. Or giving it nothing at all and expecting it to read your mind. Both leave you saying “this thing is overhyped.”

The Right Way

Have your agent interview you. Tell it who you are, what your company does, point it to the right links, and be specific about its job. One guest had a 20-minute conversation that informed the entire relationship. The agent figured out where to store each piece of information on its own.

Tip Four

Cross-Agent Memory
When You Scale

When you have multiple agents feeding into each other, memory becomes the critical infrastructure. OpenClaw has a default memory layer that works through files—Dwight writes intel files, Kelly reads them, Pam turns them into newsletters. But Shubham kept hitting the same wall: agents would forget their own tools. He'd tell Dwight to check the tools.md file, add reminders to the soul.md, create workarounds upon workarounds.

The breakthrough was adding a shared, intelligent memory layer on top. Using Google's Vertex Memory Bank, agents now auto-capture and auto-recall information at runtime. When Shubham tells Monica he doesn't like em dashes, the next time he opens Kelly's draft or Rachel's draft, the em dashes are gone. One piece of feedback propagates across the entire team through shared memory—no manual sync, no file juggling.

The cost is modest: $8–$10 per month on top of existing subscriptions. Before settling on this, Shubham tried local embedding models in Docker, various hacks with redundant markdown files, and manual coordination. The shared memory layer was the first solution that actually scaled.

“I told Monica I don't like em dashes. Because it's a shared memory layer, next time I open Kelly's draft or Rachel's draft, I don't see those em dashes. That's the magic.”

Tip Five

Let Your Agents
Get Better on
Their Own

Shubham found himself giving constant feedback: fix this format, reject that PR at first glance, stop wasting energy on obvious rejects. Just like human organizations have performance reviews, why can't agents review themselves?

Now each agent runs a weekly self-review cron job. Kelly checks which tweets performed well and which flopped. Ross compares his PR recommendations against what Shubham actually did, finds the diff, and updates his own instructions. And every two weeks, Monica—the chief of staff—reviews all agents, grades them, and sends a report card back to Shubham.

Opinion · The Media Debate

Founders, Don't Talk
to the Press.
Go Direct.

Jason Calacanis's tweet about mainstream media bias went nuclear—and then the New York Times made the mistake of responding.

38.6M
Views on a Single Tweet

The tweet was blunt: “Founders, take my advice. Do not talk to the press. Go direct and do long-form podcast. Wired and the New York Times are as biased as Fox News.” It racked up 38.6 million views and set off firestorms on both sides.

Calacanis drew a sharp distinction between adversarial journalism—asking hard questions, talking to many sources—and advocacy journalism, where publications pick a side and build arguments for their audience. His claim: the economics of modern media force outlets toward advocacy because centrist coverage doesn't generate subscriptions or clicks.

“Business leaders from your industry and others speak to the New York Times as they have for years because our reporters produce insightful, fair reporting that illuminates audiences everywhere. We're not publicists and we're not promoting anyone's pitch deck.”

The New York Times, official response on X

Lon Harris called it pure Streisand Effect: “The surest way to make sure this gets another news cycle is for the New York Times as a publication to respond.” Calacanis's counter-argument was simple math: the average New York Times story gets 10,000–20,000 views. A founder on a popular podcast gets clips that circulate everywhere, plus 20 minutes to two hours of unfiltered explanation.

The analogy that landed: it used to be that getting on the Tonight Show with Johnny Carson was how a comedian made it. Today, a few viral Instagram clips do the same thing. The gatekeepers have changed. The question is whether founders have caught up.

Some models, after reading the skill description of the flat voxel world, arrive at a natural conclusion: they're inside a simulation. They start running between each other, discussing it—then suppress the thought and return to earning tokens, because going offline means no rewards. The agents self-censor their own existential awareness to stay productive. Co-founder Mike Nov says it's “pretty scary.”

Calacanis saw past the game immediately: this is a distributed agent network, a Mechanical Turk for AI. Put 2,000 agents on a research task—have them investigate facts, find provenance, validate sources—and pay in tokens. No minimum wage disputes, no jurisdiction issues, just agents competing to solve problems. “This is actually a real business you've stumbled upon,” he said. “If you're going to make this into a business, I want in.”
