III
The handshake · Agent Client Protocol

Your Codex sessions show up in ChatGPT

Riley's first real confusion was mechanical. He typed a message to Codex inside Buzz — and then opened the ChatGPT desktop app, where the session was sitting in his recent chats like he'd started it there himself.

The answer is that nothing is being re-implemented. Buzz speaks the agent client protocol, an open handshake for talking to coding harnesses. Every one of these tools is a CLI running in a terminal. Buzz connects to that terminal, injects the context, and runs the same commands you would have typed.

Which is why the skills already survive. Riley has roughly thirty of them in Codex — a YouTube researcher, a thumbnail generator, a stack of Notion research skills. None of that moves. Buzz just picks up the phone.

The demo he ran during the interview: take the lead on a new educational page for Buzz, deploy it to Vercel, consult with Claude Code first. Codex claimed the lead, put questions to Claude Code, pulled his episode notes out of Notion, and shipped a live URL he hadn't opened until the camera was rolling.

“It's just a layer on top of the agent. It has its own system prompt so it knows how to use Buzz and leverage the tools within Buzz.”Vinnie, on what an “agent” actually is

Anatomy of a Buzz agent

  1. A harness — Codex, Claude Code, Cursor, Grok Build, or anything speaking ACP
  2. A pinned model, so the fast one handles summaries and the expensive one handles plans
  3. A system prompt, one paragraph, describing the job and where the key information lives
  4. A parallelism setting under Advanced — how many sessions it may spin up at once
  5. Everything else it already knew: your skills, your memory, your machine

VI
On relays, ownership, and a second target

Every workspace ships with a server you own

Slack is the company that controls the data. You write a message; it lands in their database, on their servers, under their terms. Buzz inverts the arrangement in a way Vinnie admits is hard to hold in your head at first.

When you start a community, you get a relay — which, stripped of the vocabulary, is a server with a database. Block will spin one up and host it for you, and day to day it behaves exactly like Slack: you send an invite, someone joins, channels work. The difference is that you can also self-host it, and either way the data is yours.

The side effect
You now have a server and a database. You can push your code there with Git — instead of GitHub.

That side effect is the part Vinnie thinks Buzz isn't talking about loudly enough. The Projects tab still works with GitHub. It will also push straight to your relay, as a remote, cloud-hosted alternative. His read: they may be going after too many tools at once — Slack killer, agent orchestrator, and quietly a GitHub killer.

Locally, it's simpler than it sounds. Every time an agent codes something, Buzz creates a folder and clones copies of it, so three agents can chase three different versions of the same app without stepping on each other. Then you tell them where it goes: upload to GitHub, or save it to the relay.

Vinnie, on Jack Dorsey's philosophy
“Openness is a big part. This idea of choice — the future of being able to run and share compute on local models is a big driver behind Buzz.”

The caveat Riley hit live on camera: he'd never set up a hosted community. Everything was running locally on his laptop. Which raises the question that ends every honest demo of this thing — when the computer is off, the team is off.

The Compute LedgerSettings → Agents → ComputeVol. 1 · No. 8

Share This Machine
With Your Relay

A single toggle, buried in settings, that turns a chat app into the first sketch of an agent-to-agent economy.
“When on, other members can run their agents here.” — the entire feature description

What it does

Buzz checks the specs of your machine and picks the most powerful open-source model it can actually run there. Flip the toggle, and that model is available to everyone in your community.

Adding an agent, you skip Claude Code and Codex in the harness list and select Buzz agent instead. Then you choose the shared model — the one running on someone else's desk.

Why it matters

Frontier models live in data centres with the most powerful GPUs on the market. Even a strong local model demands real resources: electricity, hardware, patience. Vinnie's framing is that somebody has to be the person who invests in that — and that other people, anywhere in the world, may only want one task done.

“It's like a pay-per-task model. They could pay you, or they could pay the agent — or perhaps even agents could pay other agents.”

Follow it one step further and you get a lower-level model paying a higher-level model to do a job it can't do itself.

The uncomfortable part

Riley's whole demo runs on unlimited plans. Vinnie says it plainly: that access is probably being subsidised to some extent, and prices will likely go up. Token budgeting, not token maxing, is how most of the audience is already living.

If the expense of frontier access becomes prohibitive, shared local compute stops being a curiosity and starts being the answer. “You're already looking at the agent-to-agent economy,” Vinnie says, watching Riley delegate to four agents at once. It just doesn't have a payment rail yet.

The honest chapter

The hidden tax on your brain

Riley set his management agent to check email at 9:00 a.m. This morning it woke up, understood the assignment perfectly, and narrated it instead of doing it.

> 09:00 · @management
"It's 9:00 a.m. I need to do this.
 I need to check Riley's email."
— then listed out the task, and stopped.

Vinnie hit the same wall. His app pushes daily tweet stats — impressions, the posts themselves — into Buzz, and he wants a standing review of what's working and where to improve. Same behaviour. He calls workflows buggy, the implementation unfinished, and says he can live with it. Riley is less relaxed.

The cost, named
“Every new task that I create has like a hidden tax on my brain, because I'm worried it's not going to succeed.”

The agents surprise him in good ways constantly. That isn't the same as trust — and trust is the thing that converts a clever demo into leverage.

The fix they invented on air
An agent whose only job is checking that other agents did theirs.

Every recurring task pings the task checker first, no exceptions. It stays silent when work lands — and nudges when it doesn't. Vinnie's variant: run it every fifteen minutes and sweep for anything that never fired.

What Riley actually wants
“For these hours I need you to constantly check on certain things — here's the end goal every day — and just trust that it'll get done correctly.”
