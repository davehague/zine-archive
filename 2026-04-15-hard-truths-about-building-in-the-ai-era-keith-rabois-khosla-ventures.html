II
Framework

Barrels & Ammunition

Most companies raise money, hire aggressively, and then the CEO gets frustrated: burn rate climbs but output stays flat. The fundamental driver is that the number of people who can independently drive an initiative from inception to success is extremely limited.

The ratio of barrels to ammunition dictates the number of important initiatives that can be pursued simultaneously. Hiring more people without expanding your barrel count just stacks bodies behind the same projects—increasing your collaboration tax while producing less.

III
First Principles

The Team You Build Is the Company You Build

Vinod Khosla told Rabois at Square: the team you build is the company you build. People get distracted with the market, customers, product, technology—ultimately it's the team. If you have the right people, everything else will be easy.

"If a founder shows the ability early in his or her career to assess talent ruthlessly and accurately, he or she can go very far with no other abilities whatsoever."

Hiring is a muscle. You need to exercise it, learn what worked, what didn't, what you missed and why. Tony Xu at DoorDash does 20 references on every single senior hire. Greylock's rule: you haven't exhausted referencing until you hit a negative reference.

Undiscovered Talent

Peter Thiel taught Rabois on a jog around the Stanford campus: you've got to find undiscovered talent—that's the only way to scale against large incumbents with infinite money.

The net effect is you wind up skewing younger—not because you need young people, but because younger people have fewer data points. The black-box hiring machine can't process them accurately, which is where the alpha lives.

It's like a salary cap. When you're a startup, you probably have one-tenth the salary cap of the people you're competing with.

IV
Contrarian Take

Stop Talking to Your Customers

For consumer and SMB products, customer feedback is not just unhelpful—it's actively harmful. Customers don't know what they want. Purchase decisions are subconscious, and when people try to consciously explain a subconscious decision, they give misleading information.

"Ask anybody who drives a Porsche or a Lamborghini why they bought the car. 99% of the time they will tell you every reason except the real reason."
Directionally Dangerous for Sub-Mid Market

When it works

Hardcore enterprise customer development—where there's a decision maker making a utilitarian decision. If you have 30 must-win accounts, you can talk to all 30 and influence the CEO at each one. That is a useful exercise.

When it destroys

If you're targeting a billion people on the planet, you are not getting representative feedback. Once you hear customer feedback, you can't take it out of your brain. Every subsequent meeting is polluted.

Everything important you need to learn about humans was written by Shakespeare. Just read Shakespeare. That's better than all the customer research.

VI
Origin Story

The Litigator Who Learned to Stop Counting Hours

Before PayPal, before Square, before Khosla Ventures, Keith Rabois spent four and a half years as a law clerk and litigator. The training shaped his mind in ways both useful and dangerous. Law school exams are fundamentally about issue spotting—you get credit for identifying everything that can go wrong. Moderately useful, but not useful as an entrepreneur. As a lawyer, your productivity is measured by hours billed. It took Rabois nearly two years after switching to tech to stop writing down every half hour of every meeting in his notebook.

Peter Thiel was smart enough to quit after five months and four days. David Sacks never practiced at all—"maybe even smarter than both of us."

But the legal training gave Rabois something most VCs lack: the ability to assess legal and regulatory risk in his own brain. It's no accident that many of his best investments—YouTube, Stripe, Square, Ramp—operate in heavily regulated sectors where that risk assessment creates an edge.

"The hardest part of writing a brief—all the art and all the magic—was the first paragraph. I might spend the first week walking around the office thinking through how to nail the first three sentences. Once I figured that out, I could write 30 pages in two days."

He sees AI writing the same way: the power-through-the-rest-of-the-brief works well. You still need the first three sentences.

Leadership

The Relentless Application of Force

The better you're doing, the more the CEO should push.

A friend of mine asked Mike Moritz: what's the most common denominator of the best CEOs ever? His answer: the relentless application of force. People eventually get comfortable, complacent. The more success you have, the more complacent the organization tends to get.

"It's kind of like winning a Super Bowl. Last four quarters were wonderful. You got to come back next year and start your record zero-zero again."

When a company's struggling, Rabois is actually a very non-critical coach and supporter—because the founder already knows they're struggling. Being critical doesn't help solve problems. But when the company's thriving, it's really important to be critical and isolate things that will eventually be problems.

It's like a really good sports coach. When you're winning is when to polish everything and master the details.

Really talented people are like superb athletes. When things are going well and people are coasting, they're not happy. They have an internal tempo. They want to create things and drive value.

"The morale actually does go down for the best people in the world when people are skating."

Brian Chesky was the same way: pedal to the metal no matter what. And when the team had little breaks, morale actually went down.

IX
Forecast

The Binary Sort of Content

AI content surpassing human content is inevitable. On Chinese TikTok, it's already all AI videos—and people actually enjoy watching them. But Rabois predicts a bifurcation.

Two Poles

Curation: A curated experience with a premium on provenance. This piece of art is a Warhol. Anybody could create it now in high fidelity, but there's still an appreciation that it was created by Andy Warhol.

Algorithm: A rank filtering of what's the best content, regardless of whether it's AI-generated or not.

"The alpha is in design. Just like in marketing, it's not the tools, the channels, the metrics—it's the storytelling. How do you cut through the clutter in the snappiest, most compelling possible way."
The Expanding Middle

As AI makes it easier to build, it expands from the middle out. What remains: figuring out what to build on one end, and distribution—getting anyone to pay attention—on the other.

The Ugly Baby Thesis

When making a seed investment, Rabois wants half of his VC friends to laugh at him. If they're laughing, it's an ugly baby—and ugly babies are where the real alpha lives. A 30-40% hit rate is golden. By definition, 60-70% fail.
