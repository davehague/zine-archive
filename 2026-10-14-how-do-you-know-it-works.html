Act I · The problem · Chapter 1 of 9

You can't read it anymore

Where this fitsEverything later in this issue answers one problem. With ordinary software you find out what it does by reading it. With an agent, reading stops working. Here are three things you used to be able to read, and why you can't now.

1 · The code

Read the traces instead

“With traditional software, you can read the code and you can tell with some degree of reliability what exactly that code is going to do. With agents, you have no idea.”

“Your code isn't the source of truth for what does my agent do. Instead, the traces are your source of truth.”

Laurie Voss · Arize AI

▶ 1:38 · ✂ cut · fit 60
2 · The reasoning

It isn't step by step

Eric Bigelow of Goodfire, which studies what goes on inside models: DeepSeek's R1 will “say wait 50 times or more in a single reasoning chain, that's a lot of epiphanies to have when you're working through one problem.”

“It's almost like a soup of tokens they create and then they just go back and skim out something out of that soup.”

In his words, “reasoning is almost like a misnomer.”

▶ 3:40 · ✂ cut · fit 42
3 · The language

It drifts out of English

Reward a model only for its final answer and “there isn't necessarily an incentive to keep things human readable.” So “they might as well speak to their sub agents in like weird nonhuman languages.”

“If you optimize for chain of thought being monitorable then sometimes the chains of thought are less faithful.”

Bigelow, again. Force the reasoning to look readable and it may only look sensible.

Act I · The problem · Chapter 2 of 9

It always sounds right

Where this fitsChapter 1 said you can't see inside. This chapter adds the catch: the output never tells you when it's wrong, and the rarer the failures get, the less ready you are for one. That's why the rest of the issue is about checking, and about who pays when a check is missed.

The tone · Tariq Shaukat, Sonar

“AI always gives you very high conviction plausible output.”

“It never says I think the answer is this. It always just says, the answer is this.” So the human job becomes knowing “where do I need to poke,” the way you learn where to look with a new intern.

▶ 2:30 · ✂ cut · fit 52
The odds · Nathan Labenz, Cognitive Revolution

Misbehaviour is falling with each model generation while the length of tasks AI can take on rises. Multiply them: “Problems will be really rare, but when they do hit, they might be like quite severe.” His worry is “people kind of forgetting that we even need to be able to take control back because it seems to be working so reliably.”

▶ 2:52 · ✂ cut · fit 54
The bill · Abhi Arya, Reducto

Building for agents, he says, “comes down to one question, and that is who pays for the agent being wrong.” His three answers:

Auto mode“When it messes up, which it will, you won't have any idea because it's really confident about what it's doing.” Your users pay.
User-firstEvery knob goes to the user, and the agent “can't really do much, which is basically just a chatbot experience.”
Agent experience in service of the userRich, scoped capabilities, “and even where the human can still verify the work that it's doing.” The one he thinks wins.

Act II · Looking · Chapter 3 of 9

Look at the data

Where this fitsAct II is checking, in four steps: the data, the judge, the judge's judge, your own benchmark. It starts with the raw data, the outputs, traces and documents themselves, because that is where you learn what “good” means before you ask anything else to measure it.

● Anchor · from your queue

Hamel Husain's tier list

He ranks every eval habit from S to F. The top tier is all one idea.

SError analysis: “a structured approach to looking at your data quickly.”
SReading traces yourself: “There's nothing more valuable than looking at the data.”
CWriting evals before building: “infinite surface area of things that can go wrong.”
DEvals that always pass: “they're not telling you anything at all.”
▶ 2:37 · ● queue anchor · fit 74
30%stale files →80%

Leo Platzer, formerly of Deasy Labs: if 30% of a thousand files are stale or duplicated, “it can happen that up to 80% of your actual context your agent uses to answer a question is filled up with stale information.” Cleaning it “pretty much” doubled recall and lifted task completion “between 10 and 15%.” And “duplication and freshness is true no matter what.”

▶ 3:14 · ✂ cut · fit 38
1

Read data, not code

Swyx, of AI Engineer and Latent Space, on who is worth hiring now: “people who look at data rather than code are more valuable these days.” That means “here's the logs, here's the traces, here's the schema,” and turning what you capture into an eval.

2

Because the code won't show it

His own bug: messages loaded on one refresh and not the next. Two coding agents, working at different times, had built two code paths with a race condition between them. “A human would never do that.” Reading every line won't save you; knowing what the whole system does will.

▶ 3:26 · ✂ cut · fit 52
3

Users are data too

Kent C. Dodds on putting a rough prototype in front of early adopters and watching what they do: “The problems that are really worth solving are the ones where people will just run through the glass cutting themselves and they're like, ‘We made it.’”

V
Act II · Looking · Chapter 4 of 9

Every judge has a type

Where this fitsChapter 3 ended where looking by hand runs out. The usual next step is an LLM as judge: one model grading another's output. This chapter covers the judge's known biases, and the single change to the question that helps most.

01

Position

Shown two options, it “tends to favor whichever comes first.” Some models prefer the last, “but it always has some kind of preference to position.”

02

Length

“LLMs like longer responses.” They score them higher “even if the… extra length is just filler.”

03

Confidence

Chapter 2 again: “The judge gets fooled by a response that sounds confident just like humans do.”

04

Self-preference

“If you use the same model to do the generation as you use to do the judging, they tend to like their own output.”

Laurie Voss's fix for the last one: a judge from another family. He graded Haiku with Sonnet, “but it works even better if you use a different labs model entirely.”

▶ 1:20 · ✂ cut · fit 50
39%

“Pointwise is not which dish do you want. Pointwise is do you want food, yes or no.”

Tejas Kumar · IBM

Ask a judge to pick the better of two and you invite the position bias above. Ask it yes or no about one answer instead. In a study he cites, pairwise judging “was 39% more vulnerable to attack vectors than pointwise,” while pointwise “fell apart just like five times.”

VI
Act II · Looking · Chapter 5 of 9

Who checks the checker?

Where this fitsThe judge from Chapter 4 is a model, so it needs what any model needs: a baseline to compare against and data it has never seen. Three cut clips on how to tell whether a judge, or any small model you train, is actually good.

Humans are the baseline, and humans are bad at this too. Laurie Voss: two experts with the same output and the same rules disagree a surprising amount; human inter-rater reliability “is often as low as” 0.2 or 0.3. A judge that beats that is doing better than people.

“What you're looking for is a judge that disagrees with you all of the time. That is the danger signal.”

One that disagrees some of the time “is a judge correctly handling a genuinely ambiguous task.”

He closes with a principle he credits to Anthropic's eval teams: failures should seem fair. “If you look at a failing trace and you think that answer looks fine to me, the problem is probably the eval and not the agent.”

▶ 1:20 · ✂ cut · fit 54

The second test is data the model has never seen. The AI Automators, fine-tuning a small model on about nine thousand support chats, call the split “the most important part,” and it happens “before you train anything.” Four piles:

5,900 train
~1k quiz
1k calib.
1k test

Train on the first. Quiz after every pass and keep the best version, because a run “can actually go backwards.” Tune confidence on the third. The fourth plays “no part in anything until the very end.”

“If you test a model on chats it's already trained on. It'll just remember the answers.”

▶ 1:15 · ✂ cut · fit 46

When is a small trained model worth it, as a judge or a decider? The same host: it “fits very well anywhere you make the same call over and over again where the available choices are reasonably stable and you have past examples of the right answer.” Trained on your own data, a 420-million-parameter model can beat a large general one.

Act II · Looking · Chapter 6 of 9
The Benchmark GazetteLast stop in Act II

Your benchmark, not theirs

Where this fitsThe last step of looking zooms out from one judge to choosing whole models. A public benchmark is someone else's data, graded someone else's way. Four cut clips on what that misses, and how cheap your own benchmark has become.

11 daysNathaniel Whittemore's estimate of the average gap between frontier model releases. Each arrives with a benchmark table.

The tables leak

Not because anyone is lying: “most of these benchmarks are now in the training datasets,” and “the longer they've been around, the less value there is.” Even a perfect table misses fit: “there are going to be times, I guarantee where the thing that is technically state of the art on some benchmark is worse at the version of the thing that you're doing.”

“There's kind of no shortcut solution to figuring out how a new model is or isn't going to fit into your process other than using it.”

▶ 1:28 · ✂ cut · fit 45

“Solved” means the benchmark

Pushmeet Kohli of Google DeepMind on what AlphaFold actually beat: “someone got a structure, deposited it in the PDB, and we are trying to replicate and get the same structure. That's what we did, right? And it just so happens that it's useful. But that does not mean that we have understood all of protein dynamics.” Proteins, he says, “are not blocks.”

▶ 2:12 · ✂ cut · fit 50

Good enough is a real answer

Nufar Gaspar, asked how open models compare in her testing: “some of the tasks are table stakes,” like email drafting or basic research, and there “you will not be able to see any noticeable difference.” Her verdict: “open source is good enough for most knowledge work task, especially if the harness in which it's working is a good one.”

▶ 1:02 · ✂ cut · fit 38

Now the agent builds it

A Cognitive Revolution co-host on how far delegation has come since January. His whole request about upgrading embedding models: “Please go look at all the embeddings models… make a benchmark if you know based on our own use cases, and then test all these things and give me a report.”

Write down what right means

Where this fitsAct II checked outputs after the fact. Act III moves the check earlier. Runtime Verification's guests on The Cognitive Revolution argue that writing down intent, the specification, is the part of programming that stays human, and that the act of writing it finds bugs by itself.

Act III · Proving · Chapter 8 of 9

Run the loop

Where this fitsHere the argument widens beyond software. When an idea can be checked cheaply, you can try thousands of them; when checking needs the physical world, everything slows to its pace. Four cut clips from mathematics, materials science and biology say the same thing: speed comes from how fast you can check.

Why AI cracked maths and not trucking

David Friedberg on the All-In Podcast, after OpenAI's batch of new proofs, each from under three hours of compute. AI works in loops:

an idea→test it→read the result→change the idea↺

Mathematics is the ideal case, “because you can actually test your prediction on the compute. You don't have to go do anything in the physical world.”

All in silicon

Maths. Loops run in parallel, and each “can connect dots that humans otherwise might spend many days or years trying to find.”

A physical step

Drug discovery, materials, trucking. “The speed of the loop is actually gated primarily by the physical world step.”

▶ 4:02 · ✂ cut · fit 54
30,000 tried
30 interesting

A Periodic Labs researcher on Latent Space, on how magnesium diboride, the highest-temperature conventional superconductor at ambient pressure, was found: a Japanese group “tried 30,000 different things. 30 of them seemed to host interesting superconductivity.” It wasn't theory. Automate the trying, and “we just really increased the surface area for luck.”

▶ 2:08 · ✂ cut · fit 52

“The experiment is this little tiny linear probe, which has like eight bits or something of output.”

The same team: reality holds far more than any instrument reads, so “any data we collect is very precious and we don't delete any of it.”

▶ 1:14 · ✂ cut · fit 44

The problem comes first

Pushmeet Kohli on the old machine learning habit: “here is some training data. Here is some test data. And we will just optimize the model. And that is broken.” His reading of the bitter lesson is to stop being “religious” about method. “The problem comes first.” Sometimes that means modelling, sometimes collecting the data.

Act III · Proving · Chapter 9 of 9

Measure what matters

Where this fitsThe ending brings the argument back to where it started. Once you can check, the question is what to count and where to spend the effort. Three cut clips, and the answer each time is outcomes, beginning with the data.

1

Data quality

Stale knowledge base? “No amount of prompt iteration is going to help.”

2

Prompting

Few-shot examples, explicit constraints. “Often the highest ROI.”

3

Model choice

Can solve what prompting can't, but it “will also cost more and be slower.”

4

Temperature, top-p

Easy to tweak, seldom a big lever. “The last thing you try.”

▶ 1:02 · ✂ cut · fit 58
Outcomes, not tokens

Davis Palmie of Factory: “Lines of code and tokens generated are gameable metrics.” Token leaderboards are “Goodhart's law in real time,” the same trap as the readable-reasoning one in Chapter 1. Count signal-to-production time, human interventions, median time to repair, shelf life of code, cost per PR. “Don't token max.”

▶ 1:38 · ✂ cut · fit 58
Outcomes, not certainty

Jake Van Clief to a student who had tripled his company's revenue and still felt he was building the wrong thing: “Put pressure on am I getting an outcome and am I building it in a way where I can fix it later.” “Outcomes matter more than is it right.”
