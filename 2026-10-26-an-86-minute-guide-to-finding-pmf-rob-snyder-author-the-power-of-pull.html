II
02 — The Framework

Four letters. One coiled spring.

Pain points, value props and design partners are not what make people buy. A certain kind of priority does: somebody trying to get something done right now who can’t do it with their existing options.

“Pull describes a person who would be weird not to buy.”

P

Project

A job to be done. Not “I want to buy recruitment software” but “I want to hire more employees.”

U

Unavoidable

A thing I have to do right now. Why now? Why not later?

L

List of options

Hiring someone, the existing team, tools I already have, tools on the market.

L

Limitations

Why those options are not good enough to get the project done.

V
05 — Where Ideas Come From

Earn the secret.

These things don’t emerge from a whiteboard. Rob’s friend from school had no product idea at all, only a wish to serve small businesses. He listed industries, then shadowed them, working alongside people for days at a time. Solar panel installers: “he hated them and they hated him.” Independent physical therapists: he loved them and they loved him.

He watched over their shoulders for days. He even tried to buy a physical therapy franchise so he could see the P&L. Somewhere in there he noticed: it looks like you’re trying to do X and are stuck. He wrote it up as a pull hypothesis, showed it to a therapist (“Is this you?”) and heard, “Oh my god, yeah, that’s exactly the thing.”

Then came the step that mattered: five more physical therapists, to make sure it repeated. They pulled too. The deeper reason is philosophical: if you don’t have firsthand experience, how will you make the thousands of decisions it takes to build something that serves them?

Where Rob’s 50 ideas came from

10%
90%

10% own experience. 90% going and getting firsthand experience, doing the job alongside the customer.

The two-slide test

Slide 1: “Here’s what I hear. You’re trying to do X right now because of Y; you’ve considered this but it’s not good enough for this reason. Do I have that roughly right?”

Slide 2: a conceptual overview of how you could help.

calls, then stop and check in. Did anyone lean in?

If you have pull, they’ll rip it out of your hands.

A
Pull-shaped action“I need this right now. How do we move forward right now?”
C
Pushing into the future“I could totally see us using this in Q4.”
F
Leaned-out enthusiasm“Tell me more.” “Could you send more information?”
  1. Book 30 minutes. Smaller blocks don’t register on a calendar and people don’t show.
  2. Open with: “Why did you take the call with me today?”
  3. Get a thumbs-up on the agenda, then ask: “Do you mind if I ask a few questions?”
  4. Pitch small: “It sounds like you’re looking for X without the hassle of Y. Roughly right?”

07 — Special Edition

Break Glass in Case of Emergency

Hail MaryGoblin ModeNo Design Partners

20

“Is there anything on your to-do list that you would pay me to do right now?” A founder booked 20 conference meetings and said: you tell me what to build.

The Hail Mary

When the call goes lukewarm, ask the open question

Not every call has pull. So throw the Hail Mary: is there anything you’d pay somebody to take off your plate? One startup selling an applicant tracking system into governments heard, “Yeah, that’s kind of interesting.” Then, after the Hail Mary: what they’d actually pay to solve was a job description problem. That became their first contract and first customer, and they’ve grown fast since.

“I’m just seeing if they need help with anything they are stuck on.”
Goblin Mode

Zoomies, but for founders

“You know when dogs get the zoomies?” That is Rob’s image of goblin mode: somewhat crazy things to get in front of customers right now. A conference in Europe tomorrow? Get on a plane. Tiro, a dental hygienist staffing company, had the founder’s parents go door to door to dental offices. It worked. Break glass in case of emergency, because “staying stuck is awful.”

The Pivot

Five banking calls, nothing. One intro, everything.

A startup did five calls into banking and felt nothing. One contact said it didn’t seem relevant but suggested defense companies. They knew nothing about defense. In the first conversation: “Holy, are you serious?” They changed their pull hypothesis.

Sell, Don’t Pilot

The design partner trap

A design partner “buys into your vision,” gives feedback, and never buys. The feedback is just them “playing Steve Jobs.” People with something truly important to get done “don’t want to be a design partner. They just want that thing done.” No pilots, no proofs of concept, unless the buyer insists.

On price: early on, test willingness to transact, not to pay. Frame by the Ls: the value of the finished project, or the cost of the alternatives, such as $50,000 a year for consultants. When you need a big number, show a case study: projects from $80,000 up to a million. “You tell me how you’re thinking about this.”
