Chapter V — Why Pairing Works

Two models, raised on different texts, make a better team than one that argues with itself.

The central claim is almost suspiciously simple. These models have different training and different blind spots. One catches what the other misses. The rest of the tool is plumbing.

What the author calls "arguing like co-workers" is not a metaphor so much as a description. The writer defends the choice. The reviewer flags the risk. Nobody's feelings are at stake. The diff is.

“They argue like co-workers. Of everything I've tried, this pairing works the best.”

A single model reviewing its own output tends to agree with itself. It wrote the code for reasons it still finds persuasive. A reviewer with a different intellectual lineage has no such loyalty — and, crucially, a different set of things it was taught to worry about.

The result, in practice, is a quieter but sharper workflow. Fewer bugs ship. Fewer tests go missing. Fewer secrets end up in logs. You did not hire a second engineer; you rented one for the length of a diff.

Chapter VIII — The Verdict

Of everything tried, this pairing works best.

An informal ranking, emerging from the dispatch, of what actually moves the needle when AI writes the code.

i.

Single-model self-review

Echo chamber. The author defends the author. Finds typos; misses architecture. A comfortable mirror.

ii.

Human spot-checks

Works until velocity exceeds attention. The reviewer — you — becomes a bottleneck within a week.

iii.

Static analysis only

Catches patterns. Misses intent. A linter cannot tell you the feature you shipped is the wrong feature.

iv.

Claude × Codex pairing

Two writers. Different schools. They argue. The diff gets better. This is the one.
