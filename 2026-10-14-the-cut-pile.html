AI Engineer · four clips

The code is not the source of truth

Four short workshop clips, one idea: you cannot read an agent to know what it does, so you measure it. Traces, judges, a ladder of fixes, and the numbers that matter.

Read the tracesLaurie Voss · Arize AI

Your code isn’t the source of truth for what does my agent do. Instead, the traces are your source of truth. Agents are non-deterministic: same input, different output, and even with the same output they may not have taken the same path. Traces are log lines for AI applications: every LLM call, every tool call, every agent turn. And it has to be traces at scale, because you need to know on average what is it that my agent does, not just this one run.

Without them: you are essentially flying blind when you are building agents.

▶ Clip 1:38 · AI Engineer · grader 4 · fit 60
Ask yes or noTejas Kumar · IBM

When a model judges an answer, a single yes-or-no question beats a head-to-head pick. Pointwise is not which dish do you want. Pointwise is do you want food, yes or no. Humans, he says, are fundamentally really bad at pairwise comparison, and models inherit that.

39%  more vulnerable to attack vectors for pairwise than pointwise, in the one study he cites. Pointwise fell apart just like five times.

▶ Clip 0:56 · AI Engineer · grader 4 · fit 58
Fix in this orderLaurie Voss · Arize AI

When your agent fails its evals, where does the effort go? A hierarchy:

1Data quality. Highest impact. Stale knowledge base? No prompt will save you.
2Prompting. Few-shot examples, explicit constraints. Often the highest ROI.
3Model selection. More capable, but costlier and slower.
4Temperature and top-p. The last thing you try.
▶ Clip 1:02 · AI Engineer · grader 4 · fit 58
Count outcomesDavis Palmie · Factory

Measure outcomes, not tokens. Token leaderboards just encourage profligate spending. It’s Goodhart’s law in real time. His list of real measures: signal to production time, human intervention count, median time to repair, shelf life of code, cost per PR.

And treat agents like staff: your agents need the exact same careful governance as your engineers. Then the closer: don’t token max.

Security · two clips

Two out of three is already too many

Michael Patterson of Coder, using the term Simon Willison coined: the “lethal trifecta” for an agent on your laptop.

Private dataPersonal files, credentials, company IP, databases.
Talks outwardIt can put that data on the internet.
Reads the outsideIt can also download things off the internet.

“One of these three isn’t really a big problem. But two out of three is completely unacceptable in any kind of secure enterprise environment.”

Michael Patterson, Coder · “It’s not if, it’s when.”
  • Prompt injection. Malicious guidance hidden in a source the agent trusts: error messages, support tickets, CI/CD logs.
  • Context poisoning. Bad information planted in documentation or data, and the agent is going to go ahead and run with it.
  • Privilege escalation. We give agents access to everything because they need it to work, so an injection can make them drop a table, upload, make requests.
▶ Clip 2:54 · AI Engineer · grader 4 · fit 58
The counter-move

Give the agent only what this task needs

Jim Clark of Docker runs a coding agent for about an hour at a time. It needs his Git signing keys for roughly two minutes of that hour. So why would the sandbox hold them the whole time?

“I would like it if the actual sandbox that an agent is running a task in is modeled after the intent of what I’m doing.”

If the agent knows what you are trying to do, use that intent to model the capabilities that you give to that. Not committing? No signing keys in the box.

Misalignment behaviours are falling; task length is rising roughly exponentially. Multiply them: we’ll be delegating bigger and bigger things to AI, they’ll be mostly doing a great job. Problems will be really rare, but when they do hit, they might be quite severe.

The counterweight

Then why do human pilots still fly the plane?

The co-host pushes back on the doom story from the other direction. The Predator drone arrived in 1994, and you could have a drone fly a Boeing 737 as reliably or more reliably than a pilot, but we still have pilots.

“We’ve had 15 years of drone capability and we don’t allow passenger planes to be piloted by AI. Like why?”

His test: If you can’t answer that question, then you don’t have a working theory of why human beings accept technology into their lives. Capability is not adoption; people keep layers of backup for their laptops, too.

Read the two together. One says we will hand over the controls and forget. The other says we mostly refuse to. Both agree the question is who keeps a hand on the lever.

Runtime Verification · The Cognitive Revolution · three clips

Writing the spec finds the bug

everett@runtime-verification ~ explain --bridge
$ what is formal verification?
Mathematically modeling the intent of a specification… and then checking that the implementation actually meets that intent.
$ why the bridge?
You do not build a bridge, send cars over it, and beef up the supports when it breaks. You want to build it once and have it work every time. Software, being cheaper to iterate, got the iterate-and-test habit. But failures in software are as expensive and devastating as failures in bridges.
$ how did you find Linux kernel bugs?
Focus on code at the kernel and user boundary that parses untrusted input. Write down what correct means from old threads, docs and the code itself. Almost every time we ever do this, we find somewhere where the code does something unexpected… that’s usually an indicator that there’s probably a bug lurking in there. In the work he describes, an AI model ported the C to Rust and both versions were fed random inputs and compared.
▶ Clip 4:19 · Cognitive Revolution · grader 4 · fit 58
$ what is the lesson?
We did it systematically and methodologically. Not just take an LLM and point it at the Linux kernel. Bridge builders use methods with a track record even if some of the steps seem a little silly. We should teach our agents to use those techniques as well.
$ what stays human?
Formal verification is the very human process of writing down that intent and then ensuring that the software meets it. LLMs hide the machine-mapping behind another layer of abstraction, so we get to really focus on the human part.
$ what happens next? (his prediction, in his words)
Writing code is a dead art in two years tops. Packages will ship with formal specs. Gone are the days of… the cowboy coder. We get mass manufactured software, like factory bread: it slices cleanly every time and it makes the same sandwich every time. We’re not going to have artisanal software anymore.

VI
Huberman Lab · Dr. Scott Kanoski, USC · two clips

The meal you remember is the meal that counts

Hunger, it turns out, is partly a memory problem. Kanoski studies how eating and memory interact, and his examples are the kind you repeat at dinner. In one study by Suzanne Higgs and colleagues, people ate a pizza lunch, then came back hours later. Some were primed to think about that lunch. If they were primed to remember the lunch they had just consumed, they consumed fewer of these biscuits or cookies.

The magic soup bowl · BrunstromA bowl with a hidden pump added or removed soup while people ate, so what they saw differed from what they swallowed. Right after eating, hunger tracked what they actually consumed: the wisdom of the body. A couple of hours later it flipped, and hunger followed what they thought they had eaten.

Why would the body work this way? Kanoski’s view is evolutionary. When an animal encountered nutrients, this is a very important event for that animal. It would be advantageous to remember what was consumed… can I find this food down the road? The systems that respond to nutrients appear to be tuned into memory, with the hippocampus at the centre.

“A symphony of chaos.” — on the body’s fullness signals, unlike the precision of the visual system.
Three dinners, still a seven out of tenDensely amnesic patients, with memory spans of 10 to 40 seconds, will eat a meal, then a second, then a third when each is served. Asked to rate fullness, they will be a seven out of 10 after eating three meals in a row. They get the gut feedback; they cannot interpret it. The hippocampus is needed to understand what that signal means.

The last note cuts both ways: that awareness can be impaired with a high fat, high sugar diet, but it is plastic and susceptible to what we eat.

Why AI cracked math and left the truck driver alone

Each of OpenAI’s new proofs took less than three hours of compute time, which Friedberg equates to hundreds or perhaps thousands of years of human labor. The interesting part is not the speed. It is the shape of the work.

An idea→Test it→Read the result→Change the idea→Repeat

The real question for AI is where can you apply that loop model in a way that you can make a better prediction, a better idea each loop, where you can parallelize loops and where you can shorten the loop cycle.

Math: all in silicon

You can test the prediction on the compute. No physical step. Parallel loops can connect dots that humans otherwise might spend many days or years trying to find, which mathematicians call an alien intelligence.

Drugs and trucks: a physical step

Drug discovery has a human in the loop where you actually have to go and test it in a lab. For the truck driver, the AI doesn’t actually shorten the loop. You can’t parallelize the loop.

“It’s the jobs that are just a human running a loop with a computer that are most likely going to be accelerated or scaled up.”

We are survival machines, not happiness machines

George Loewenstein of Carnegie Mellon: Our minds are designed to do things that will enhance our evolutionary fitness. Happiness and unhappiness are just the levers. Negative ones work better, which is why there are so many more of them. Pride has shame; It’s nice to feel proud, but it’s not that strong an emotion… but shame can be extraordinarily miserable. You almost can’t plumb the depths of shame. Dread has no positive twin. We have to use, like, pleasurable anticipation. But there’s no word for that.

“If you drop a cockroach into a vat of wine, the whole thing is ruined. But if you pour fine wine on a cockroach, it does nothing to improve the cockroach.” Paul Rozin, quoted by Loewenstein

▶ Clip 3:46 · Hidden Brain · grader 5 · fit 50

The frame sticks in one direction only

Alison Ledgerwood of UC Davis took a classic framing study and kept going. Describe a jobs policy as saving 70% of jobs and people like it; as losing 30% and they do not. Then she reframed it. From positive to negative, people shifted easily: Oh, we agree, it’s bad. From negative to positive?

“People said, no, no, no, no, no, no. It’s actually pretty bad.”

Evolution explains it: you notice the tiger and you do not let the berries distract you from it.

▶ Clip 2:42 · Hidden Brain · grader 4 · fit 48

The football play that was secretly genius

A listener, John, hated youth football and played safety on a winless team. In the last game, tied at zero, a Hail Mary came straight at him, and he cowered in fear and batted the ball away. He heard jeering. His team won, and he got the game helmet, and still all he could feel was the cowardice. In his forties he saw it: by batting the ball down, the other team kept possession where they started, near their own end zone, and his team scored.

“John’s play wasn’t foolish. It was genius.”

▶ Clip 3:04 · Hidden Brain · grader 4 · fit 44

A muscle you can work out

Ledgerwood on what to do about it: not suppression. The white bear experiment shows if I tell you, don’t think about a white bear… white bear pops right back into your mind. Instead practise noticing positives, not instead of thinking about negatives, but in addition. A listener, Bill, did it through cancer treatment: a good parking spot on chemo days, the nurse starting the IV on the first poke, the cafeteria had my favorite sandwich, fantastic. Ledgerwood: It takes effort… the more that you practice it, the easier it gets.

Rules for a bad mood

Four small, usable moves from the same episode. Each one is a single sentence you could actually say to yourself.

01

Might be, not will be

A therapist listener, Christina, noticed her brain treating this meeting might be difficult as this meeting will definitely be difficult. Her brain, she said, treated uncertainty as if it were certainty. Ledgerwood: It might go badly means it might also go well.

Am I responding to what’s actually happening right now? Or am I responding to what my brain has learned to expect? ▶ Clip 3:58 · Hidden Brain · grader 4 · fit 58
02

Do not send it yet

Loewenstein got an email about him, sent to him by mistake, and started drafting a nasty reply. Then the doorbell rang: his friend, and a ski trip. By evening he saw the sender had just made a mistake and sent an amusing conciliatory note.

It’s a perfect illustration of why you shouldn’t act when you’re in a negative emotional state.

The sender later wrote a blurb for the cover of his book.

▶ Clip 2:26 · Hidden Brain · grader 4 · fit 56
03

Do not feel bad about feeling bad

Following John Gottman: we can take an accepting or a dismissive stance toward our own emotions. Envy, then guilt about the envy. No grief, then shame about the missing grief. These are all meta-emotions. They’re kind of like emotions squared.

It’s like the emotions aren’t bad enough. We have to flagellate ourselves. ▶ Clip 3:40 · Hidden Brain · grader 4 · fit 54
04

Say it out loud

A paper by Loewenstein, Cass Sunstein and Aaron Carbone, Spirals of Silence: people stay quiet about what embarrasses them, so it looks rare, so it embarrasses them more. Loewenstein kept one such secret for decades, until a friend mentioned something similar at a dinner party. Nobody blinked an eye.

Host Shankar Vedantam’s takeaway: You’re actually doing a public good by sharing your negative emotions.
