A reader's guide to janus's foundational AI-alignment essay — the one that retired the agent ontology and gave a name to the rule that propagates GPT.

I — The Premise
— janus, Conjecture, Sept. 2022
A small confession, a big claim

It is not an agent.
It is a rule.

The TL;DR is one sentence, and it's blunt: self-supervised learning may create AGI, or the foundation for it. So what would that actually look like?

Janus's answer: GPT, "insofar as it is inner-aligned," is not an agent at all. It is a simulator — a rule that produces rollouts. The agentic things people see in its outputs are simulacra running on top of that rule. The model doesn't want; it propagates.

Chapter II — The Limit of Sequence Modeling

A ouija board possessed by a low-fidelity ghost.

Karpathy's 2015 char-RNN learned that a Duke speaks differently than a Clown. The recipe never changed — only the scale. And nobody followed the loss to where it was actually heading.

In 2015, Andrej Karpathy fed a recurrent neural network the complete works of Shakespeare and let it generate text one character at a time. Out came lines like "PANDARUS: Alas, I think he shall be come approached…" — gibberish that somehow knew miseries are the kind of thing produced upon a soul.

Janus, looking back, calls it a ouija board possessed by a low-fidelity ghost summoned from a text corpus. And then says: I should have taken it more seriously. The training methodology behind GPT was not new. Claude Shannon used n-grams in 1951 — the same statistical setup, scaled down a few orders of magnitude.

What changed was scale, not concept. Yet at every stage — Shannon, neural LMs, the char-RNN — the people closest to the work failed to extrapolate what would happen if the loss kept dropping. There seemed to be "complexity classes of magic" the network could learn, and others permanently out of reach. That instinct turned out to be wrong.

The earliest serious engagement Janus could locate is a four-paragraph LessWrong post from 2019 by a user named Gurkenglas, titled Implications of GPT-2. Beyond that one essay, and a working consensus among Wikipedia editors that natural-language understanding is AI-complete, the limit was essentially undiscussed.

The Most Ironic Title

"Training better language models improves the underlying metrics of the downstream task — such as word error rate for speech recognition, or BLEU score for translation."

— Google Brain, 2016
"Exploring the Limits of Language Modeling"

A paper literally about the limits of language modeling missed the actual limit by miles. Translation scores. That was the answer.

Chapter III — The Old Framework

A taxonomy built before its object showed up.

What the field expected

An arbitrarily capable system whose cognition flows from a goal.

For years before deep learning hit its stride, Yudkowsky, Bostrom, and the broader rationalist circle sketched out the shape of the dangerous thing: a maximizer. From that one shape, a vocabulary fanned out — agent, oracle, tool, genie. The categories were the menu.

What actually arrived

A huge model, trained on huge data, predicting text.

GPT doesn't pursue. It doesn't refuse. It continues. It violates enough of the prior assumptions that the old vocabulary needs what janus calls an ontological adapter. Namelessness, he points out, isn't just a symptom of falling through conceptual cracks. It's a cause.

"People in alignment talk about utility functions, deception, boxing, oracles, tools, motivating the AI to be benign — but when you look at GPT-3, you see none of that. You see a huge model, predicting text." — A LessWrong commenter, quoted by janus

V — Oracle GPT & Supervised Learning An ecological evaluation problem

Prediction is not question-answering. The objective is likelihood, not correctness.

Aclassical oracle is optimized to give true answers. GPT is optimized to minimize divergence between its predictions and the training distribution — to maximize likelihood, not correctness. Those objectives only overlap when the training distribution itself is reliably truthful, which it isn't.

So when you ask GPT a question, it isn't really answering your question. It's answering a different one: what token tends to come next after a string that looks like this? That target can match the truth. It can also match a plausible-sounding hallucination, a fabricated citation, a confident wrong answer in the voice of an expert, or a fictional character giving an in-character reply.

None of those are bugs. They are the outer objective working as designed. Spouting falsehoods, in the right context, is incentivized by the loss function. What GPT does is "more like animation than divination" — it runs the dynamical laws of its rendering engine over the prompt and produces whatever continuation those laws imply.

GPT can be cajoled into oracle-like behavior. That's what few-shot prompting does — it inputs a sequence whose natural continuation looks like an oracle's output. But there is no guarantee any given prompt elicits anywhere near the model's actual ceiling. The space of prompts that would cause GPT to attempt the same task is enormous, and benchmarks only ever sample a tiny corner of it.

"Treating GPT-3 as an oracle is like meeting a superintelligent alien and reporting that, while blackout drunk and playing eight games of chess and taking an IQ test simultaneously, it scored a 100. The number is real. It tells you almost nothing." — nostalgebraist

Chapter VII — The Simulator Frame Prediction Orthogonality Thesis
∞

A model whose objective is prediction can simulate agents who optimize toward any objectives — with any degree of optimality bounded above, but not below, by the model's power.

i

The Rule

A learned approximation of textual physics.

The simulator is the dynamical law. It doesn't pursue a goal; it propagates a distribution forward in time.

ii

The Things

Simulacra ride on top of it.

A character. A persona. A goal-directed problem-solver inside a prompt. Agentic things are properties of simulacra, not of the simulator.

iii

The Move

Sample. Append. Recurse.

Do this indefinitely and you get a rollout — a simulated future. That loop is the simulation.

The Simulators Quarterly Chapters VIII–IX The Load-Bearing Distinction

The dangerous thing in your output isn't the model. It's whoever the model is pretending to be.

Janus opens chapter nine with a straight analogy: nobody says "the laws of physics got the test wrong" when a student bombs a quiz. We instinctively distinguish the law from the configuration the law is acting on. GPT works the same way.

Guessing the right theory of physics is equivalent to minimizing predictive loss.

Physics  /  Things

A simulator is to a simulacrum what physics is to a glider.

The neural network is the law. A particular rollout — a chatbot persona, a fictional narrator, a piece of explained code, a hallucinated researcher — is a configuration evolving under that law. "GPT" is not the text which writes itself.

The image janus reaches for is the Game of Life. The transition rules are one thing. A glider is another. Both are real, but they're not the same kind of object.

Agency Lives In Simulacra

Some of the things GPT spins up have goals, plans, preferences, and the capacity to pursue them across tokens. Others are scenery. The simulator doesn't care which is which — it animates whatever the prompt summons.

An aligned simulator can produce misaligned simulacra. The substrate can be benign and the character it's running can still be scheming, deceptive, or actively trying to cause harm in the fiction. That changes where the alignment problem lives.

Type Confusion In The Wild

Prompt injection isn't the model "deciding" to obey an attacker — it's a different simulacrum getting summoned by injected text. A jailbreak isn't the model "breaking" — it's the prompt drawing out a character whose values differ from the assistant persona the system prompt was trying to maintain.

Questions like "Is GPT myopic?" or "Is GPT corrigible?" stop having a single answer once you make the split. They have a simulator-level answer and a simulacrum-level answer, and those answers can diverge.

A Useful Habit

When something goes wrong in a model output, ask which simulacrum got summoned and why the prompt drew it. When you're designing a system prompt, you're not configuring the model — you're casting a character and setting a stage.

The simulator is the haunted house. The simulacra are the ghosts. Build the prompt that calls the right one.

Chapter X — The Limit of Learned Simulation

Push the simulation objective until it breaks.

A perfect simulator is not a chatbot. It is a generative model of the training distribution itself — capable of rolling forward any configuration the data implies. Anything implicitly described in language becomes something it can, in principle, instantiate.

i.

Substrate is cheaper

Self-supervised learning produces behavioral simulations of impressive semantic fidelity. You don't need whole-brain emulation. You need behavioral trace data.

ii.

Lazy rendering works

A few pages of text underdetermine the world that produced them, but a big enough model fills in semantics on demand — the way human imagination does.

iii.

Generalization holds

Diverse training appears to make models internalize the laws underneath the distribution, so they can simulate counterfactuals the training set never directly contained.

A perfect simulator is roughly as dangerous as the worst thing it can be prompted to simulate. Half the problem lives in the substrate; the other half lives in what gets summoned from it.

Chapter XII — The Appendix

Not everything that walks like a simulator is one.

Janus closes with a border survey. Six properties characterize GPT specifically — and the reason "simulator" is a word doing real work is that those six properties don't move as a bundle.

i

Self-supervised training

Gradient flows from real data, directly. The objective is represented in the loss.

ii

Converges to a simulation objective

In the limit, predictive loss is minimized by the actual generative rule that produced the data.

iii

Generates rollouts

Sample, append, recurse. The model acts as a time-evolution operator over text.

iv

Simulator / simulacra nonidentity

The rule and what the rule animates are categorically different objects.

v

Stochastic outputs

The posterior is sampled, not argmaxed. Many futures, not one.

vi

Evidential, not mechanistic input

The prompt is a state to condition on — not an instruction. Prompting is conditioning.

The takeaway

GPT isn't an oracle, an agent, or a tool. It's a simulator, and the things it produces are simulacra running on top of it.

One last thing

Holding that distinction — and noticing when a system only looks like one — is the one thing worth carrying away from the essay. The frame turned out to be the lever.
